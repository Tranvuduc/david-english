A1 — Mới bắt đầu

1. Động từ be: am, is, are

Động từ be (là, thì, ở) thay đổi theo chủ ngữ. I đi với am. He, She, It đi với is. You, We, They đi với are. Câu phủ định thêm not sau be: I am not a teacher. She is not from Hanoi.

  • I am a student. — Tôi là học sinh.
  • She is Vietnamese. — Cô ấy là người Việt Nam.
  • They are my friends. — Họ là bạn của tôi.
  • We are not teachers. — Chúng tôi không phải là giáo viên.

Trong bài: Chào hỏi và giới thiệu bản thân · Luyện ngay: Gõ câu

2. Hỏi tên và giới thiệu bản thân

Hỏi tên: What is your name? (Tên bạn là gì?). Trả lời: My name is + tên. Hỏi quê quán: Where are you from? (Bạn đến từ đâu?). Trả lời: I am from + nơi chốn. Chú ý: What is viết tắt là What's, I am viết tắt là I'm.

  • What is your name? — My name is Minh. (Tên bạn là gì? — Tên tôi là Minh.)
  • Where are you from? — I am from Hanoi. (Bạn đến từ đâu? — Tôi đến từ Hà Nội.)
  • Nice to meet you! — Nice to meet you too! (Rất vui được gặp bạn! — Tôi cũng vậy!)

1. Sở hữu cách: 's

Để nói một người hay một vật thuộc về ai, thêm 's vào tên người đó: Lan's mother = mẹ của Lan; Nam's father = bố của Nam. Với tên kết thúc bằng s, chỉ thêm dấu phẩy trên: James' book = sách của James.

  • Lan's mother is a teacher. — Mẹ của Lan là giáo viên.
  • This is Nam's sister. — Đây là em gái của Nam.
  • My father's name is Hung. — Tên bố tôi là Hùng.

2. Tính từ sở hữu: my, your, his, her, our, their

Tính từ sở hữu đứng TRƯỚC danh từ và không bao giờ thêm s: my mother (mẹ của tôi), his father (bố của anh ấy), her brother (anh trai của cô ấy), our family (gia đình của chúng tôi), their house (nhà của họ). Không nói 'the my mother' — chỉ dùng một từ sở hữu.

  • This is my family. — Đây là gia đình của tôi.
  • Her brother is a student. — Anh trai của cô ấy là học sinh.
  • Our parents are teachers. — Bố mẹ của chúng tôi là giáo viên.

1. some và any

some dùng trong câu khẳng định và lời mời: I have some bread. Would you like some tea? any dùng trong câu phủ định và câu hỏi: I don't have any milk. Do you have any eggs? Với danh từ đếm được số ít, dùng a/an: an egg, a cup of tea.

  • I have some rice. — Tôi có một ít cơm.
  • I don't have any coffee. — Tôi không có cà phê.
  • Would you like some juice? — Bạn có muốn uống chút nước ép không?
  • Do you have any bread? — Bạn có bánh mì không?

2. I like / I don't like

Nói sở thích: I like + danh từ hoặc động từ thêm ing: I like tea. I like eating noodles. Phủ định: I don't like + danh từ/ing: I don't like fish. Câu hỏi: Do you like coffee? — Yes, I do. / No, I don't.

  • I like chicken. — Tôi thích thịt gà.
  • I don't like fish. — Tôi không thích cá.
  • Do you like tea? — Yes, I do. — Bạn có thích trà không? — Có.
  • She likes fruit, but she doesn't like meat. — Cô ấy thích trái cây, nhưng không thích thịt.

1. There is / There are

There is + danh từ số ít: There is a table in the kitchen. There are + danh từ số nhiều: There are two chairs in the room. Câu hỏi: Is there a garden? — Yes, there is. Are there any windows? — Yes, there are.

  • There is a sofa in the living room. — Có một ghế sofa trong phòng khách.
  • There are two bedrooms in my house. — Có hai phòng ngủ trong nhà tôi.
  • Is there a garden? — Yes, there is. — Có vườn không? — Có.
  • There isn't a TV in my bedroom. — Không có ti-vi trong phòng ngủ của tôi.

2. Giới từ chỉ vị trí: in, on, under, next to, between

in = trong, on = trên mặt, under = dưới, next to = bên cạnh, between = ở giữa hai vật: The book is on the table. The cat is under the chair. The lamp is next to the bed. The table is between the sofa and the window.

  • The book is on the table. — Quyển sách ở trên bàn.
  • The cat is under the chair. — Con mèo ở dưới cái ghế.
  • The kitchen is next to the living room. — Nhà bếp ở bên cạnh phòng khách.
  • The lamp is between the bed and the window. — Cái đèn ở giữa giường và cửa sổ.

1. Hỏi và nói giờ

Hỏi giờ: What time is it? (Mấy giờ rồi?). Trả lời bắt đầu bằng It is... Giờ chẵn: o'clock (3 o'clock = 3 giờ). 30 phút: half past (half past five = 5 giờ 30). 15 phút: quarter past (quarter past nine = 9 giờ 15); 45 phút: quarter to (quarter to eight = 8 giờ kém 15, tức 7 giờ 45).

  • It is three o'clock. — Bây giờ là 3 giờ.
  • It is half past five. — Bây giờ là 5 giờ rưỡi.
  • It is quarter past nine. — Bây giờ là 9 giờ 15 phút.
  • It is quarter to eight. — Bây giờ là 7 giờ 45 phút (8 giờ kém 15).

2. Các thứ trong tuần và giới từ on

Hỏi thứ: What day is it today? Trả lời: It is + thứ trong tuần. Khi nói làm việc gì vào thứ nào, dùng giới từ on trước thứ: on Monday, on Friday. Các thứ trong tuần luôn viết hoa chữ cái đầu: Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, Saturday, Sunday.

  • What day is it today? — It is Monday. (Hôm nay là thứ mấy? — Hôm nay là thứ Hai.)
  • I go to school on Monday. (Tôi đi học vào thứ Hai.)
  • We have English on Tuesday and Friday. (Chúng tôi học tiếng Anh vào thứ Ba và thứ Sáu.)

A2 — Cơ bản

1. be going to: kế hoạch tương lai

be going to + động từ nguyên thể nói về kế hoạch đã quyết định: I am going to travel to Da Nang. She is going to stay at a hotel. Câu hỏi: Are you going to visit Hue? — Yes, I am.

  • I am going to travel to Da Nang next month. — Tôi sẽ đi du lịch Đà Nẵng vào tháng tới.
  • She is going to stay at a hotel. — Cô ấy sẽ ở khách sạn.
  • Are you going to visit Hue? — Yes, I am. — Bạn có định thăm Huế không? — Có.

2. Thì quá khứ đơn: kể chuyến đi đã qua

Quá khứ đơn kể chuyện đã xảy ra: We visited Ha Long Bay last year. Động từ có quy tắc thêm ed (visit thành visited, stay thành stayed); động từ bất quy tắc đổi dạng (go thành went, buy thành bought, take thành took). Câu hỏi: Did you have a good trip? — Yes, I did.

  • We visited Ha Long Bay last year. — Năm ngoái chúng tôi đã thăm vịnh Hạ Long.
  • She bought two tickets yesterday. — Hôm qua cô ấy đã mua hai vé.
  • Did you have a good trip? — Yes, I did. — Bạn đã có chuyến đi vui vẻ chứ? — Có.

1. Thì hiện tại đơn nói về thói quen

Thì hiện tại đơn (Present Simple) dùng để nói về thói quen hằng ngày và sự thật hiển nhiên. Với chủ ngữ I, You, We, They: động từ giữ nguyên. Với chủ ngữ He, She, It: động từ thêm s hoặc es (get thành gets, watch thành watches, study thành studies). Câu hỏi: Do you get up early? — Yes, I do. / Does she get up early? — Yes, she does.

  • I get up at six o'clock. — Tôi thức dậy lúc 6 giờ.

  • He watches TV in the evening. — Anh ấy xem ti-vi vào buổi tối.
  • They go to work at eight. — Họ đi làm lúc 8 giờ.

2. Trạng từ tần suất: always, usually, often, sometimes, never

Trạng từ tần suất nói bạn làm việc gì thường xuyên đến mức nào: always (luôn luôn, 100%), usually (thường thường, 80%), often (thường, 60%), sometimes (thỉnh thoảng, 40%), never (không bao giờ, 0%). Vị trí: đứng TRƯỚC động từ thường (I usually get up) nhưng đứng SAU động từ be (He is always late).

  • I always get up at six. — Tôi luôn thức dậy lúc 6 giờ.
  • She usually has breakfast at home. — Cô ấy thường ăn sáng ở nhà.
  • He is always late. — Anh ấy luôn đi muộn.
  • We never watch TV in the morning. — Chúng tôi không bao giờ xem ti-vi vào buổi sáng.

1. Hỏi giá: How much is it?

Hỏi giá: How much is + danh từ số ít? (How much is this shirt?). Hỏi giá số nhiều: How much are + danh từ số nhiều? (How much are these shoes?). 2. this/that/these/those

this (cái này) và these (những cái này) chỉ thứ ở GẦN người nói. that (cái kia) và those (những cái kia) chỉ thứ ở XA người nói. Số ít dùng this/that + is; số nhiều dùng these/those + are. Ví dụ: This is my bag. (Đây là túi của tôi.) That is your book. (Kia là sách của bạn.)

  • This shirt is cheap. — Cái áo này rẻ.
  • That shirt is expensive. — Cái áo kia đắt.
  • These shoes are beautiful. — Những đôi giày này đẹp.
  • Those pens are blue. — Những cây bút kia màu xanh.

B1 — Trung cấp (Kingdom English)

Present perfect: since/for, already/yet; present perfect vs past simple

Structure: have/has + past participle — I have read; she has studied; they have lived. The present perfect connects the past to NOW. Use it for a past action with a result that matters today, or for an experience in your life up to now. Rule 1: since + a point in time; for + a length of time I have believed in Jesus since 2018 (a point: 2018). — We have studied the Sermon on the Mount for three weeks (a length: three weeks). Other points: since Monday, since I was a child, since last year. Other lengths: for five years, for a long time, for two days. Rule 2: already (affirmative) and yet (negative and question) I have already read the Beatitudes twice this week. — She has already memorized Matthew 5:3. I have not memorized them yet. — Have you read the Sermon on the Mount yet? Already goes before the main verb; yet goes at the end. Rule 3: Present perfect vs past simple — finished time or not? Use the past simple when the time is FINISHED: Jesus taught the Sermon on the Mount about two thousand years ago. — They visited the church last Sunday. Use the present perfect when NO finished time is given and the result matters now: I have read the Beatitudes twice this week. — Have you ever been to a Bible study on a mountain? Signal words for the past simple: yesterday, last week, in 2019, ago. Signal words for the present perfect: ever, never, already, yet, since, for, this week (the week is not finished).

Past perfect (had + past participle); past perfect vs past simple in narrative

Form: had + past participle — had chosen, had waited, had already looked, had given. The past perfect describes the earlier of two past actions. Think of a timeline: the past perfect is the action that happened first, further back in the past; the past simple is the action that happened second, closer to now. Structure 1: “By the time + past simple, … had + past participle” “By the time Samuel arrived, Jesse had already presented seven sons.” — Samuel’s arrival is the later past moment; Jesse’s presenting happened before it. “By the time Goliath fell, David had thrown only one stone.” Use already before the participle to stress that the earlier action was complete: had already chosen, had already promised. Structure 2: “After / Before” with two past actions “David repented after Nathan had told him the truth.” (Nathan’s telling came first.) “Samuel anointed David after God had rejected Saul.” With after, the past perfect follows it; with before, the past perfect comes first: “Before David became king, he had looked after his father’s sheep for many years.” Structure 3: Past perfect vs. past simple — don’t mix them up Past simple = a finished action in the past: “David killed Goliath.” Past perfect = an action finished before another past action: “David had killed a lion and a bear before he fought Goliath.” In a story, use the past simple for the main events and the past perfect only to “step back” and explain what had happened earlier. If you use the past perfect for everything, your story sounds wrong — use it like a flashback, not like the main plot. Common mistakes: (1) Forgetting had: “God chosen David” — wrong; say “God had chosen David.” (2) Past perfect for a single past event: “David had killed Goliath yesterday” — wrong; say “David killed Goliath.” (3) Base verb after had: “had choose” — wrong; say “had chosen.”

Passive voice (present/past simple passive; passive with by)

Form: be + past participle. Present simple: am / is / are + past participle — "Forgiveness is given freely." Past simple: was / were + past participle — "He was wounded for our transgressions." The by-phrase names the agent: "He was despised by men." — "Our iniquity was laid on Him by the Lord." Active to passive: move the object to the front, and the subject goes to the end with by (or disappears). Active: "Men despised Him." Passive: "He was despised by men." Active: "God invites the thirsty." Passive: "The thirsty are invited by God." When to use the passive — three reasons: 1. The receiver matters more than the doer: "He was wounded for us" — the focus is on the Servant, not on who wounded Him. Bible English loves the passive for this reason. 2. The doer is unknown or unimportant: "The invitation was written in Isaiah 55" — who wrote it does not matter here. 3. The doer is God, and the focus is on grace: "We are saved by grace" — the gift matters, not our part in it. Watch out: do not use the passive when the doer is the point: "Isaiah wrote these words" is better than "These words were written by Isaiah." And never forget the be verb: say "He was wounded", not "He wounded".

Relative clauses: who / which / that; defining vs non-defining

A relative clause is a small sentence inside a sentence. It begins with a relative pronoun — who, which, or that — and it describes a noun: "The friend who gave me wise counsel is a true friend." "The lamp which guides my feet is God's word." "The path that leads to life is narrow." Which pronoun? Use who for people: "the man who meditates on God's law". Use which for things: "the law which makes wise the simple". Use that for people or things: "the counsel that you gave me" / "the tree that stands by the water". Defining clauses (no commas) tell us which one — the information is essential. "The friend who tells the truth is a treasure." (Which friend? The one who tells the truth.) Without the clause, we do not know which friend is meant. Non-defining clauses (with commas) add extra information about someone we already know. "Solomon, who was the son of David, asked God for wisdom." (We already know who Solomon is; the clause is a bonus. You can remove it and the sentence still works.) Note: that is never used in a non-defining clause — say "Solomon, who was the son of David", never "Solomon, that was...". Watch out: never use which for a person ("the man which meditates" is wrong — use who), and never drop the pronoun when it is the subject of the clause: "The man who meditates on God's law is blessed" — not "The man meditates on God's law is blessed".

Second conditional (If + past simple, would + base verb); wishes and hypotheticals

Form: If + past simple, would + base verb. "If we were justified by works, grace would not be grace." — "If Abraham had been justified by works, he would have something to boast about." Use were (not was) after if for all persons in formal English: "If I were still under the law…" ("If I was…" is common in speech, but were is correct in writing.) Meaning: the second conditional talks about unreal or imaginary situations — things that are not true now, or that we are only imagining. Paul uses it to show why salvation cannot be by works: "If we were justified by works" — but we are not! We are justified by faith. Compare the first conditional (real future): "If you believe, you will have peace" — with the second (imaginary): "If you believed, you would have peace." Wishes — the same past-simple form after wish: I wish + past simple — a wish about the present that is not true: "I wish I had peace with God." (I do not have it yet.) — "I wish I trusted Christ more." — "I wish I were sure of my salvation." Note: wish takes were too: "I wish I were free from fear." Would in the result clause: the main clause always uses would (not will): "If we trusted in our works, we would never know if we had done enough." Short forms in speech: "If I trusted Him, I'd have peace." Watch out: never use would in the If-clause: say "If we were justified by works…", not "If we would be justified…". And never use will in the result: say "…grace would not be grace", not "…grace will not be grace".

Trong bài: Bài 5: Rô-ma: Được Xưng Công Chính Bởi Đức Tin · Luyện ngay: Gõ câu

Reported speech: statements (say/tell + that-clause, tense backshift), questions (ask + if/whether/wh-word), commands (tell/ask + to-infinitive)

When we tell someone what another person said, we usually shift the tense back one step — because the words were spoken in the past. This is called reported speech (or indirect speech). Structure 1: Reported Statements — say / tell + that Direct: Jesus said, “The kingdom of heaven is like a mustard seed.” Reported: Jesus said that the kingdom of heaven was like a mustard seed. (In Bible retelling, the present tense is often kept when the truth is still true: “Jesus said that the kingdom of heaven is like a mustard seed” is also correct.) Use say with no person, and tell with a person: “He said that he would return.” — “He told his servants that he would return.” Never say “He said me that…” Backshift table — direct speech to reported speech: Present simple to past simple: “The seed is the word of God.” to “The teacher said that the seed was the word of God.” Present continuous to past continuous: “The thorns are growing.” to “He said that the thorns were growing.” Will to would: “I will return.” to “The master said that he would return.” Can to could: “I can explain it.” to “Hoa said that she could explain it.” Present perfect to past perfect: “I have found the treasure.” to “He said that he had found the treasure.” Structure 2: Reported Questions — ask + if / whether / Wh-word Yes/No questions use if or whether, and the word order becomes statement order (no do / does / did, no question mark): Direct: “Is the kingdom like a pearl?” Reported: “Lan asked if the kingdom was like a pearl.” Wh-questions keep the question word: Direct: “What does the parable mean?” Reported: “The disciple asked what the parable meant.” Structure 3: Reported Commands — tell / ask + person + to + verb Direct: “Watch and be ready.” Reported: “Jesus told the disciples to watch and be ready.” Direct: “Use my money well.” Reported: “The master told his servants to use his money well.” For a negative command, use not to: “Do not be afraid” to “He told them not to be afraid.” Watch out: do not keep the question word order in reported questions — say “He asked what the parable meant”, not “He asked what did the parable mean”. And remember: say takes no person (“He said that…”), tell always takes a person (“He told me that…”).

Trong bài: Bài 6: Các Dụ Ngôn Về Nước Thiên Đàng · Luyện ngay: Gõ câu

Phrasal verbs: come upon, fill with, speak out, stand up, pour out, break out; separable vs inseparable, with the object pronoun rule

A phrasal verb is a verb + a small word (a particle, like up, out, upon) that together have a new meaning. Pour means to tip liquid from a container; pour out means to give freely and in full measure — God poured out His Spirit. The six phrasal verbs of this lesson all come from Acts: come upon — to arrive and rest on someone: "The Holy Spirit came upon the believers." fill with — to make full of something: "God filled them with boldness." stand up — to rise to your feet: "Peter stood up and spoke." speak out — to speak boldly and openly: "Do not be afraid to speak out about Jesus." pour out — to give freely and in full measure: "God poured out His Spirit on all people." break out — to start suddenly: "Great joy broke out in the city." Separable phrasal verbs — the object can go in the middle pour out and speak out are separable: the object can go between the verb and the particle, or after the whole verb. Both are correct: "God poured out His Spirit on all people." — "God poured His Spirit out on all people." And: "They spoke out the word boldly." — "They spoke the word out boldly." Inseparable phrasal verbs — nothing goes in the middle come upon, fill with and break out are inseparable: the object must come after the whole verb, never in the middle. Say "The Spirit came upon them", never "came them upon". Say "God filled them with power", never "filled with them power". Say "Joy broke out in the city", never "broke in the city out". stand up usually takes no object at all: "Peter stood up." The object pronoun rule — the most important rule When the object is a pronoun (it, them, him, her, us), the rule becomes strict. With a separable verb, the pronoun must go between the verb and the particle: "God poured it out on all people." — never "poured out it". "They spoke it out boldly." — never "spoke out it". With an inseparable verb, the pronoun stays after the whole verb: "The Spirit came upon them." — "God filled them with power." Watch out: Vietnamese has no phrasal verbs, so learners often put the pronoun in the wrong place ("poured out it") or split an inseparable verb ("came them upon"). Ask two questions: (1) Can this verb split at all? (2) Is my object a pronoun? If the verb splits and the object is a pronoun, the pronoun goes in the middle — always.

Trong bài: Bài 7: Đức Thánh Linh Trong Sách Công Vụ Các Sứ Đồ · Luyện ngay: Gõ câu

Modals — advice (should / ought to), obligation (must / have to), possibility (might / may / could); prohibition (mustn't) vs no obligation (don't have to)

Modal verbs (should, ought to, must, have to, might, may, could) show the speaker's attitude: is this good advice, a duty, or only a possibility? The modal is always followed by the base verb (no -s, no to) — except ought to, which keeps its to. The form never changes for he / she / it: “He should pray”, not “He should prays”. Structure 1: Advice — should / ought to (something is a good idea) “We should pray every day.” — “We ought to worship God with a sincere heart.” — “You should thank God for His goodness before you ask for more.” Ought to is a little stronger and more formal than should, and it is common in Bible teaching. Note the form: should + base verb but ought to + base verb. Never say “You should to pray” or “We ought worship”. Structure 2: Obligation — must / have to (something is necessary) “True worshipers must worship the Father in spirit and truth.” (John 4:24) — “Christians have to gather together to worship God.” (Heb 10:25) Must is the speaker's own strong command — Jesus Himself uses it in John 4:24. Have to is an obligation that comes from outside — a rule, a situation, or someone else. In the negative they are very different: mustn't means “do not do it” (“We mustn't worship with a proud heart”), but don't have to means “it is not necessary” (“You don't have to pray for a long time — a short, sincere prayer is precious”). Structure 3: Possibility & Permission — might / may / could “God might answer our prayer in a way we do not expect.” — “You may come to the prayer meeting tonight if you are free.” — “You could begin with a simple prayer.” Might shows a small possibility (less sure than may); may gives permission; could offers a gentle suggestion or a possibility. All three take the base verb: “God might answer”, not “God might answers”. Modal strength — from weakest to strongest: might / could (maybe) — may (permission / possibility) — should / ought to (good advice) — have to (necessary, from outside) — must (necessary, the speaker's command). Watch out: never use to after should, must, might, may, could; only ought to and have to keep the to.

Trong bài: Bài 8: Cầu Nguyện Và Thờ Phượng · Luyện ngay: Gõ câu

Gerunds and infinitives: verb + -ing (enjoy, love, stop + -ing) vs verb + to-infinitive (want, decide, hope, called us to love); verbs with meaning change: stop, remember

A gerund is a verb with -ing used as a noun: “Serving together is a joy.” An infinitive is to + verb: “God called us to love one another.” Some verbs are followed by a gerund, some by an infinitive — and the choice is fixed, so you must learn which verbs take which form. Structure 1: Verb + Gerund (-ing) Use the gerund after these verbs: enjoy, love, hate, like, finish, practise, stop, start, keep, mind, suggest, avoid. Examples: “We enjoy serving together.” — “She loves encouraging her friends.” — “He stopped worrying about his gift.” After a preposition, always use the gerund: “Thank you for helping me.” — “We grow by serving together.” Structure 2: Verb + to-Infinitive Use the infinitive after these verbs: want, decide, hope, plan, need, learn, choose, promise, try, remember, forget. Examples: “God called us to love one another.” — “I decided to help with the Sunday school.” — “They hope to grow up into maturity.” — “We want to build up the body of Christ.” The infinitive also shows purpose — why we do something: “Christ gave gifts to equip the saints.” — “We meet to worship God together.” Structure 3: Verbs That Take Both — With a Change of Meaning Stop: stop + -ing means quit doing it: “I stopped worrying.” (I do not worry any more.) Stop + to + verb means stop in order to do something else: “We stopped to pray.” (We stopped our walk in order to pray.) Remember: remember + -ing means remember a past action: “I remember singing in the choir.” (I sang, and I remember it.) Remember + to + verb means do not forget to do something in the future: “Remember to pray for our pastors.” (Do not forget to pray.) Watch out: never say “I enjoy to serve” (use serving) and never say “I want serving” (use to serve). A useful trick: learn the verb and its form together — enjoy serving, want to serve — as one pair, not as two separate words.

Trong bài: Bài 9: Hội Thánh: Một Thân Thể · Luyện ngay: Gõ câu

Used to / would for past habits (used to + verb for habits and states; would + verb only for repeated actions; did not use to for negatives); future perfect intro (will have + past participle with by + future time)

Structure 1: Used to + Verb — Past Habits and Past States We use used to + verb for a habit or a state in the past that is no longer true now: “I used to worry about money all the time.” (I worried then; I do not worry now.) — “We used to be very poor.” (We were poor then; we are not poor now.) The negative is did not use to (note: use, not used): “We did not use to have a car.” The question is Did you use to…?: “Did you use to worry about money?” Structure 2: Would + Verb — Repeated Past Actions Only We use would + verb for a repeated action in the past: “Every Sunday, my father would count the money for the week.” — “Every month, my grandmother would save a little money.” But would is only for actions, never for states. We cannot say “I would be poor” or “I would worry about money” for a state — for states, use used to: “I used to be poor.” — “I used to worry about money.” Test: if you can add “every Sunday” to the action, would is possible; for a feeling or a state, only used to works. Structure 3: Future Perfect Intro — Will Have + Past Participle The future perfect talks about something that will be finished before a time in the future. Form: will have + past participle. It is almost always used with by + a future time: “By next year, I will have saved enough money for an emergency.” (The saving will be finished before next year.) — “By June, she will have paid all her debts.” — “By 2028, they will have helped their children through school.” Compare the three times: past habit (I used to worry), present change (now I save every month), future result (by next year, I will have saved enough). Together they tell the whole story of a changed life.

Trong bài: Bài 10: Tiền Bạc, Công Việc & Sự Thỏa Lòng · Luyện ngay: Gõ câu

Wish + past (present wishes: I wish I could speak better English; I wish the church had more workers); third conditional intro (If + past perfect, would have + past participle: If Jonah had obeyed God at first, he would have gone to Nineveh at once)

Structure 1: Wish + Past — Present Wishes Use wish + past simple to talk about something you want to be different now: “I wish I could speak better English.” — “I wish our church had more workers for the harvest.” — “I wish I knew more about the nations.” Notice: although we use the past form, the meaning is present. Could is very common here: wish + could + verb means “I want to be able to…”. Use wish + would + verb for a wish about the future, or a wish that someone else would change: “I wish God would send more workers into His harvest.” — “I wish my neighbours would listen to the good news.” Use wish + past perfect (had + past participle) for a wish about the past — something that already happened and cannot change: “I wish Jonah had obeyed God at first.” — “I wish I had studied English earlier.” Structure 2: Third Conditional — If + Past Perfect, Would Have + Past Participle The third conditional talks about an unreal past — something that did NOT happen, and its imaginary result. Form: If + had + past participle, would have + past participle. Examples: “If Jonah had obeyed God at first, he would have gone to Nineveh at once.” (But he did not obey at first — he fled to Tarshish.) — “If the people of Nineveh had not repented, God would have destroyed the city.” (But they did repent, so God showed mercy.) The two halves can change places: “Jonah would not have spent three days in the fish if he had obeyed God at first.” Use the negative forms to show the opposite of what really happened: had not fled / would not have been thrown into the sea. Watch out: never use would in the if clause — say “If Jonah had obeyed…”, not “If Jonah would have obeyed…”. And remember: wish + past is about the present (“I wish I could speak better now”), while the third conditional is about the past (“If I had studied earlier, I would have spoken better then”).

Trong bài: Bài 11: Sứ Mạng Đến Với Muôn Dân · Luyện ngay: Gõ câu

Capstone review: present simple/continuous, past simple/continuous/perfect, present perfect, future will/going to, future perfect (intro), conditionals 1-3 recap, passive recap.

This lesson adds no new grammar point. Instead, we review everything from Lessons 01–11 in one table. The question to ask about every sentence is: when does it happen, and is it finished? If you can answer those two questions, you can choose the right form. Mixing tenses in one story: “John saw (past simple) a new heaven. The first heaven had passed (past perfect) away. Jesus has risen (present perfect), so we have (present simple) a living hope today. One day, God will wipe (future) away every tear.” Practise telling Revelation 21 in four tenses: what John saw (past), what is true now (present perfect), what God will do (future), and what will already be finished by then (future perfect). Watch out: (1) never use will after if in a Type 1 conditional — say “If we trust God, He will guard us.” (2) Present perfect needs has/have — “Jesus has risen.” (3) Future perfect needs a future time marker — “By the time Jesus returns, …” or “By then, …”.

Trong bài: Bài 12: Khải Huyền và Hy Vọng Sống Động — Ôn Tập Tổng Kết · Luyện ngay: Gõ câu

B2 — Trên trung cấp (Kingdom English)

Perfect continuous tenses: present perfect continuous (have/has been + verb-ing) and past perfect continuous (had been + verb-ing)

Form. Present perfect continuous: have / has + been + verb-ing. Past perfect continuous: had + been + verb-ing. Example: I have been living here for five years. They had been waiting for the Messiah for many years before Jesus came. Use 1 — an action that started in the past and is still going on. Present perfect continuous stresses the activity and its duration, not the finished result. Use it with for (a length of time) and since (a starting point). Example: She has been studying English since 2020. We have been praying for our city all year. Use 2 — a recent activity with a visible result now. Example: I have been reading First Peter this week, so my Bible is full of notes. Your eyes are red — have you been crying? The continuous form explains the present situation. Present perfect continuous or present perfect simple? Use the continuous when you want to stress how long the activity has lasted. Use the simple when you want to stress the completed result or the number of times. Compare: I have been writing letters all morning (the activity lasted all morning) with I have written three letters this morning (the finished result: three letters). Use 3 — past perfect continuous: a longer action that was in progress before another past event. The longer action uses had been + verb-ing; the shorter, later event uses the past simple. Example: The fishermen had been working all night before Jesus came to them. The believers had been praying together when the Spirit came. Watch out — stative verbs. Verbs that describe a state, not an action, are not used in continuous forms: know, believe, belong, love, hate, understand, need, want. Say I have known him for years, not I have been knowing him for years. Say We have belonged to this church since 2019, not We have been belonging. Common mistake. Do not use the present continuous for a long duration that reaches the present. Wrong: I am living here for five years. Right: I have been living here for five years. The present continuous (I am living here now) describes only this moment; the perfect continuous connects the past to the present.

  • I have been living in Hanoi for five years.
  • She has been studying English since 2020.
  • They have been waiting for the bus for an hour.
  • We have been praying for our city all year.
  • I have been reading First Peter this week.
  • The fishermen had been working all night before Jesus came to them.

Trong bài: Bài 1: Sống Như Khách Lạ Trên Đất · Luyện ngay: Gõ câu

Conditionals type 3 and mixed conditionals: past regrets, past causes with present results, present causes with past results

Type 3 — a past that cannot be changed. Form: If + past perfect, would have + past participle. Example: If Paul had given up, we would never have read these words. Both parts refer to the past, and both are impossible now: Paul did not give up, and we did read the words. Type 3 is the grammar of regret and of thankfulness for what did happen. Mixed conditional 1 — a past cause with a present result. Form: If + past perfect, would + base verb. Example: If my mother had not taught me to pray, I would not know where to turn now. The cause is in the past (she taught me), but the result is now (I know where to turn). Use this form when a past event still shapes the present. Mixed conditional 2 — a present cause with a past result. Form: If + past simple, would have + past participle. Example: If I were stronger now, I would have helped you yesterday. The cause is present (I am not strong now), but the result is past (I did not help yesterday). This form is less common, but it is exactly what you need when you explain a past failure by a present fact. Watch out — never use would in the if-clause. Wrong: If I would have known, I would have come. Right: If I had known, I would have come. Would belongs only in the result clause. For possibility instead of certainty, use could have or might have: If he had asked, God might have given him wisdom. Using conditionals to talk about the Bible. Type 3 retells past events: If Joseph had not trusted God, he would have given up in prison. Mixed conditionals apply the past to the present: If God had not loved us, we would have no hope today. If Christ had not risen, our faith would be in vain.

  • If Paul had given up, we would never have read these words.
  • If I had studied harder, I would have passed the exam.
  • If my mother had not taught me to pray, I would not know where to turn now.
  • If I were stronger now, I would have helped you yesterday.
  • If he had saved money last year, he would be rich now.
  • If they had listened to the warning, they would not be in trouble now.

Trong bài: Bài 2: Đau Khổ và Hy Vọng · Luyện ngay: Gõ câu

Modals of deduction and speculation: must / can't / may / might / could (present) and must have / can't have / might have + past participle (past)

Present deduction — how sure are you? Must + base verb means I am sure it is true: He must be a wise man. Can't + base verb means I am sure it is NOT true: She can't have decided yet. May, might, and could + base verb mean it is possible, but I am not sure: It might be God's will, or it might not. Past deduction — looking back. Must have + past participle means I am sure it happened: They must have wanted you very much. Can't have + past participle means I am sure it did NOT happen: She can't have decided at once. Might have or may have + past participle means it is possible it happened: The news might have been good. Must for deduction is not must for obligation. Compare: You must pray (obligation — you have to pray) with He must be wise (deduction — I am sure he is wise). The same word has two jobs, and the context tells you which one it is. Do not confuse them. Choosing the strength. Must and can't claim 90 to 100 percent certainty. May, might, and could claim only possibility. Saying It must be God's will claims that you are certain about God's will — be careful with that claim. Saying It might be God's will is humbler, and usually wiser. Asking about the past. To question a past deduction, use Can he have...? or Could he have...? Example: Can he have forgotten the meeting? To soften a deduction, add Do you think...? Example: Do you think it might be God's will? Softening is a mark of wisdom in English: it leaves room for other people to disagree.

  • He listens before he speaks. He must be a wise man.
  • She is still praying. She can't have decided yet.
  • It might be God's will, or it might not.
  • They must have wanted you very much.
  • He must have been a patient man.
  • The roads are wet. It must have rained last night.

Trong bài: Bài 3: Sự Khôn Ngoan Cho Quyết Định · Luyện ngay: Gõ câu

Advanced passive: reporting verbs (It is said that... / He is said to...) and causative have / get (have something done; get someone to do something)

Reporting passive 1 — It is said / thought / believed that + clause. Use it when you report what people say in general, without naming anyone: It is said that what we watch shapes what we think. It is more formal and more careful than People say... — and it is the form you will meet in news reports, academic writing, and sermons. Reporting passive 2 — subject + is said / thought / believed to + base verb (present) or to have + past participle (past). He is said to be a wise teacher. Paul is believed to have written Philippians from prison. The second form (to have + past participle) reports a past event: She is thought to have written the letter in Rome. Causative have / get + object + past participle. You arrange for someone else to do something for you: I had my phone repaired last week. I paid someone to repair it; I did not repair it myself. Get is more informal and more common in speech: She got her hair cut yesterday. Causative have / get + person + verb. Two patterns, and they are different: have + person + base verb (I had the teacher explain the verse — no to), and get + person + to + verb (I got the teacher to explain the verse). Do not mix them: had him to explain is wrong, and got him explain is wrong. Why use the passive here? The passive lets you talk about ideas without blaming or naming people. It is thought that our habits shape our minds is gentler than People think... — useful when you discuss sensitive topics in the church, and it is the mark of a careful, B2-level writer.

  • It is said that what we watch shapes what we think.
  • It is thought that our habits shape our minds more than our wishes do.
  • He is said to be a wise teacher.
  • She is thought to know the Bible well.
  • Paul is believed to have written Philippians from prison.
  • I had my phone repaired last week.

Trong bài: Bài 4: Tâm Trí Của Cơ Đốc Nhân · Luyện ngay: Gõ câu

Noun clauses (that / what / whether / if) and cleft sentences for emphasis (It is... who/that... / What... is...)

Noun clauses — a clause that works like a noun. That-clauses follow verbs of saying and believing: Jesus said that the greatest must be the servant. Paul teaches that an overseer must be above reproach. In speech, that can be dropped: Jesus said the greatest must be the servant. Wh-clauses — what, who, why, how + clause. What matters most is character. I don't know why he wants to be a leader. Note the word order: a noun clause uses statement order, not question order. Say I don't know why he wants to be a leader, not I don't know why does he want to be a leader. Whether and if clauses. I don't know whether he will be a good leader. Whether is more formal than if. Use whether after prepositions and before infinitives: the question of whether to choose him, I don't know whether to stay or go. It-clefts for emphasis. Form: It + be + stressed part + who / that + rest of the sentence. It was Jesus who turned leadership upside down. The cleft puts a spotlight on one part of the sentence: not Paul, not Peter — Jesus. Use it when you want to correct someone or to underline the point of a sermon. What-clefts for emphasis. Form: What + clause + be + stressed part. What matters most is character. What Paul requires is an overseer above reproach. Use clefts sparingly: one cleft in a paragraph gives it power; five clefts in a paragraph make it sound strange.

  • Jesus said that the greatest must be the servant.
  • What matters most is character, not talent.
  • It was Jesus who turned leadership upside down.
  • It is the servant who is truly great.
  • What Paul requires is an overseer above reproach.
  • I don't know whether he will be a good leader.

Trong bài: Bài 5: Lãnh Đạo và Phục Vụ · Luyện ngay: Gõ câu

Discourse markers for argumentation (however, furthermore, on the other hand, in contrast, nevertheless) and concession clauses (although / even though / despite / in spite of)

Discourse markers — words that show how your ideas connect. However means but, in a formal way: Some laughed. However, some believed. Furthermore means and also, more strongly: Paul listened first. Furthermore, he proclaimed the truth. On the other hand gives the opposite view. In contrast gives the direct opposite. Nevertheless means despite that. Position and punctuation. Markers usually come at the start of a sentence, followed by a comma: However, some believed. In the middle of a sentence, use two commas: Some, however, believed. Never write however without a comma after it at the start of a sentence. Concession with although and even though. Although / even though + clause (with a verb): Although many people worshipped many gods, Paul showed them the one true God. Even though is stronger than although: it concedes something surprising. Even though some people laughed, Paul kept preaching. Concession with despite and in spite of. Despite / in spite of + noun or verb-ing: Despite being alone, Paul spoke boldly. Do not mix the two patterns: Despite Paul was alone is wrong, and Although being alone is wrong. Although needs a verb; despite needs a noun or an -ing form. Arguing gently. Use concession to agree before you disagree: Although I understand your view, I believe Christ is the only way. This is how Paul argued in Athens, and it is how Peter tells us to answer: with gentleness and respect.

  • Some laughed. However, some believed.
  • Paul listened first. Furthermore, he proclaimed the truth.
  • Although many people worshipped many gods, Paul showed them the one true God.
  • Despite being alone in Athens, Paul spoke boldly.
  • Even though some people laughed, Paul kept preaching.
  • My colleague is kind. On the other hand, she does not believe in Christ.
