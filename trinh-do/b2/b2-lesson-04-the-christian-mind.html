Ngữ pháp

Giải thích và ví dụ từ chính bài học.

Advanced passive: reporting verbs (It is said that... / He is said to...) and causative have / get (have something done; get someone to do something)

Reporting passive 1 — It is said / thought / believed that + clause. Use it when you report what people say in general, without naming anyone: It is said that what we watch shapes what we think. It is more formal and more careful than People say... — and it is the form you will meet in news reports, academic writing, and sermons.

Reporting passive 2 — subject + is said / thought / believed to + base verb (present) or to have + past participle (past). He is said to be a wise teacher. Paul is believed to have written Philippians from prison. The second form (to have + past participle) reports a past event: She is thought to have written the letter in Rome.

Causative have / get + object + past participle. You arrange for someone else to do something for you: I had my phone repaired last week. I paid someone to repair it; I did not repair it myself. Get is more informal and more common in speech: She got her hair cut yesterday.

Causative have / get + person + verb. Two patterns, and they are different: have + person + base verb (I had the teacher explain the verse — no to), and get + person + to + verb (I got the teacher to explain the verse). Do not mix them: had him to explain is wrong, and got him explain is wrong.

Why use the passive here? The passive lets you talk about ideas without blaming or naming people. It is thought that our habits shape our minds is gentler than People think... — useful when you discuss sensitive topics in the church, and it is the mark of a careful, B2-level writer.

  • It is said that what we watch shapes what we think.
  • It is thought that our habits shape our minds more than our wishes do.
  • He is said to be a wise teacher.
  • She is thought to know the Bible well.
  • Paul is believed to have written Philippians from prison.
  • I had my phone repaired last week.
  • She got her hair cut yesterday.
  • I got the teacher to explain the verse again.

Phát âm

Assimilation: sounds change when words join — ten bikes, good girl, don't you, did you, this year

What is assimilation? When words join in fast speech, the last sound of one word changes to match the first sound of the next. This is called assimilation. Ten bikes becomes tem bikes: /n/ changes to /m/ before /b/, because both sounds use the lips.

More examples. Good girl becomes goob girl: /d/ changes to /b/ before /g/. Don't you becomes doncha: /t/ + /j/ becomes /tʃ/. Did you becomes didja: /d/ + /j/ becomes /dʒ/. This year becomes thish year: /s/ + /j/ becomes /ʃ/. Last year becomes lash year.

Why does it happen? Your mouth takes the shortest path between two sounds. Changing /n/ to /m/ before /b/ is easier than keeping /n/, because your lips are already closing for /b/. These changes are normal in natural speech: native speakers do them without thinking, and they do not even notice.

You do not have to copy every assimilation, but you must recognise it when you hear it. Listen for it in the lesson audio: don't you, did you, ten bikes. Then try it yourself, slowly at first: ten bikes, tem bikes; good girl, goob girl; don't you, doncha; did you, didja.

Listen and repeat: ten bikes — tem bikes (/n/ changes to /m/ before /b/); good girl — goob girl (/d/ changes to /b/ before /g/); don't you — doncha (/t/ + /j/ becomes /tʃ/); did you — didja (/d/ + /j/ becomes /dʒ/); this year — thish year (/s/ + /j/ becomes /ʃ/); last year — lash year (/st/ + /j/ becomes /ʃ/)

Bài đọc

Paul begins Romans 12 with an appeal: I appeal to you therefore, brothers, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God, which is your spiritual worship. Worship is not only what we do on Sunday. It is what we do with our bodies, our time, and our minds every day. Then Paul tells us how change happens: Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind. The world has a pattern, and it presses us into its shape every day — through the news, through advertising, through the opinions of people around us. We do not change by trying harder in our own strength. We change when our minds are renewed, and our minds are renewed by what we feed them. That is why Philippians 4:8 gives us a filter for every thought: Finally, brothers, whatever is true, whatever is honorable, whatever is just, whatever is pure, whatever is lovely, whatever is commendable, if there is any excellence, if there is anything worthy of praise, think about these things. Eight qualities, one question: is this thought true, honorable, just, pure, lovely, commendable, excellent, and worthy of praise? If it is not, do not let it dwell in your mind. A renewed mind can do what an unrenewed mind cannot: it can discern what is the will of God, what is good and acceptable and perfect. So guard your mind the way you would guard a house. Choose what comes in through the door. Read God's word first in the morning. Meditate on what is true. And watch what happens: the mind that is renewed is the mind that is transformed.

Câu 2. 2. How are we transformed, according to Romans 12:2?

Câu 3. 3. What will a renewed mind be able to do?

Đáp án: Discern what is the will of God: what is good and acceptable and perfect.

Câu 4. 4. Name four things Philippians 4:8 tells us to think about.

Đáp án: Any four of: true, honorable, just, pure, lovely, commendable, excellent, worthy of praise.

Câu 5. 5. Why does it matter what we feed our minds?

Đáp án: Because what we think about shapes who we become — we are transformed by what fills our minds, so we must guard what we feed them.
