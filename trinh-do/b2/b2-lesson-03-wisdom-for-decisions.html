Ngữ pháp

Giải thích và ví dụ từ chính bài học.

Modals of deduction and speculation: must / can't / may / might / could (present) and must have / can't have / might have + past participle (past)

Present deduction — how sure are you? Must + base verb means I am sure it is true: He must be a wise man. Can't + base verb means I am sure it is NOT true: She can't have decided yet. May, might, and could + base verb mean it is possible, but I am not sure: It might be God's will, or it might not.

Past deduction — looking back. Must have + past participle means I am sure it happened: They must have wanted you very much. Can't have + past participle means I am sure it did NOT happen: She can't have decided at once. Might have or may have + past participle means it is possible it happened: The news might have been good.

Must for deduction is not must for obligation. Compare: You must pray (obligation — you have to pray) with He must be wise (deduction — I am sure he is wise). The same word has two jobs, and the context tells you which one it is. Do not confuse them.

Choosing the strength. Must and can't claim 90 to 100 percent certainty. May, might, and could claim only possibility. Saying It must be God's will claims that you are certain about God's will — be careful with that claim. Saying It might be God's will is humbler, and usually wiser.

Asking about the past. To question a past deduction, use Can he have...? or Could he have...? Example: Can he have forgotten the meeting? To soften a deduction, add Do you think...? Example: Do you think it might be God's will? Softening is a mark of wisdom in English: it leaves room for other people to disagree.

  • He listens before he speaks. He must be a wise man.
  • She is still praying. She can't have decided yet.
  • It might be God's will, or it might not.
  • They must have wanted you very much.
  • He must have been a patient man.
  • The roads are wet. It must have rained last night.
  • She is smiling. The news might have been good.
  • He can't have read Proverbs 3, or he would trust God more.

Phát âm

Luyện phát âm theo bài — nghe audio và đọc to theo.

Listing and contrast intonation: rising tone on non-final list items, falling tone on the last; contrastive stress on the word that differs

Lists — rise, rise, fall. In a list, the voice rises on every item except the last, which falls. Pure (rise), peaceable (rise), gentle (rise), open to reason (rise), full of mercy (rise), impartial (rise), and sincere (fall). The rise tells the listener there is more to come. The fall tells them the list is finished.

Short lists work the same way. Faith (rise), hope (rise), and love (fall). If you fall on every item, the list sounds finished too early, and the listener stops listening. If you rise on the last item, the listener keeps waiting for more.

Contrast — stress the word that differs. I asked for WISDOM, not money. She trusted GOD, not herself. The stressed word carries the meaning, and the rest of the sentence goes quiet. In a decision, the contrast is often the point: God's will, not MY will.

Practice. Read James 3:17 aloud as a list, rising on each quality and falling on the last: pure, peaceable, gentle, open to reason, full of mercy and good fruits, impartial and sincere. Then read the Friend's three steps: I will ask God for wisdom, talk to my pastor, and wait for peace. Rise, rise, fall.

Listen and repeat: pure (rise), peaceable (rise), gentle (rise), open to reason (rise), full of mercy (rise), impartial (rise), and sincere (fall); faith (rise), hope (rise), and love (fall); Trust in the Lord (rise), pray for wisdom (rise), and wait for peace (fall); I asked for WISDOM, not money.; She trusted GOD, not herself.; I want GOD'S will, not my will.

Bài đọc

Every day we make decisions, and some of them change our lives. Shall I take this job? Shall I move to another city? Shall I marry this person? The Bible does not give us a list of answers for every decision, but it does give us a way to decide. Solomon wrote the shortest guide to decisions in the whole Bible: Trust in the Lord with all your heart, and do not lean on your own understanding. In all your ways acknowledge him, and he will make straight your paths. Trusting God does not mean we stop thinking. It means we do not trust our thinking alone. We bring our plans to God, we ask him for wisdom, and we let him straighten the path. James tells us how to ask: If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives generously to all without reproach, and it will be given him. God does not scold us for asking. He gives generously, like a father who loves to give good gifts. But James adds a warning: let him ask in faith, with no doubting. The person who doubts is like a wave of the sea, driven and tossed by the wind, a double-minded man, unstable in all his ways. And James gives us a test for every decision: the wisdom from above is first pure, then peaceable, gentle, open to reason, full of mercy and good fruits, impartial and sincere. A decision that comes from God will look like that list. It will be pure, not mixed with selfish ambition. It will be peaceable, not full of quarrelling. It will be gentle and open to reason, not proud and stubborn. So when you face a hard decision, do not decide alone and do not decide in a hurry. Ask God for wisdom, talk to wise friends, and wait for peace. The straight path is the path God makes straight.
