Ngữ pháp

Giải thích và ví dụ từ chính bài học.

Conditionals type 3 and mixed conditionals: past regrets, past causes with present results, present causes with past results

Type 3 — a past that cannot be changed. Form: If + past perfect, would have + past participle. Example: If Paul had given up, we would never have read these words. Both parts refer to the past, and both are impossible now: Paul did not give up, and we did read the words. Type 3 is the grammar of regret and of thankfulness for what did happen.

Mixed conditional 1 — a past cause with a present result. Form: If + past perfect, would + base verb. Example: If my mother had not taught me to pray, I would not know where to turn now. The cause is in the past (she taught me), but the result is now (I know where to turn). Use this form when a past event still shapes the present.

Mixed conditional 2 — a present cause with a past result. Form: If + past simple, would have + past participle. Example: If I were stronger now, I would have helped you yesterday. The cause is present (I am not strong now), but the result is past (I did not help yesterday). This form is less common, but it is exactly what you need when you explain a past failure by a present fact.

Watch out — never use would in the if-clause. Wrong: If I would have known, I would have come. Right: If I had known, I would have come. Would belongs only in the result clause. For possibility instead of certainty, use could have or might have: If he had asked, God might have given him wisdom.

Using conditionals to talk about the Bible. Type 3 retells past events: If Joseph had not trusted God, he would have given up in prison. Mixed conditionals apply the past to the present: If God had not loved us, we would have no hope today. If Christ had not risen, our faith would be in vain.

  • If Paul had given up, we would never have read these words.
  • If I had studied harder, I would have passed the exam.
  • If my mother had not taught me to pray, I would not know where to turn now.
  • If I were stronger now, I would have helped you yesterday.
  • If he had saved money last year, he would be rich now.
  • If they had listened to the warning, they would not be in trouble now.
  • If God had not loved us, we would have no hope at all.
  • If Joseph had not trusted God, he would have given up in prison.

Intonation: certainty vs doubt — falling tone for confident statements, fall-rise tone for doubt and reservation

Falling tone — certainty. The voice starts high and falls to a low pitch, and it stays down. This is the music of a finished, confident statement. Use it for statements of faith: God is with us. We are more than conquerors. Nothing can separate us from God's love.

Fall-rise tone — doubt and reservation. The voice falls and then rises again, as if the sentence is not finished. This is the music of doubt, hesitation, and polite disagreement: I think he will come... She might recover... Maybe he will come tomorrow... The speaker is holding something back.

Same words, different music. Say He will come with a falling tone and you sound sure. Say it with a fall-rise tone and you sound unsure. In Romans 8, Paul's great statements are falling-tone statements: We are more than conquerors. Read them with confidence, letting your voice fall and rest.

Practice. Read the memory verse (Romans 8:28) with falling tones on the key words: we KNOW, all things, for GOOD. Then read the Friend's worried questions from the dialogue with a fall-rise tone: Even the hard things? Even my mother's illness? Feel the difference in your own voice.

Listen and repeat: God is with us. (falling tone — certain); I think he will come... (fall-rise tone — doubtful); We are more than conquerors. (falling tone — certain); She might recover... Bài đọc

Paul wrote Romans 8 to Christians who were suffering. He did not tell them that their suffering was small, and he did not tell them that God had forgotten them. He told them something far greater: the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that is to be revealed to us. Paul looked at the whole creation and saw it groaning, like a woman in the pains of childbirth, waiting to be set free from its bondage to corruption. We groan inwardly too, as we wait for the redemption of our bodies. Hope is not pretending that everything is fine. Hope is waiting with patience for what we do not see yet. While we wait, the Spirit helps us in our weakness. When we do not know what to pray for as we ought, the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words. And we know that for those who love God, all things work together for good, for those who are called according to his purpose. Paul then asked the question that every suffering Christian asks: Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? Shall tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or danger, or sword? His answer is a shout of victory: No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us. Neither death nor life, nor angels nor rulers, nor things present nor things to come, nor powers, nor height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord. James agrees. He tells us to count it all joy when we meet trials of various kinds, because the testing of our faith produces steadfastness. Suffering does not separate us from God. It proves that his love holds us, even in the dark.
